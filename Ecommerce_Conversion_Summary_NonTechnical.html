FINDINGS SUMMARY — FOR PRODUCT, CX & MARKETING

Can we tell which shoppers are about to buy?

Short answer: yes, well enough to act on. We looked at two months of real shopping activity to find out whether a shopper who puts something in their cart — but doesn't buy — can be spotted early and nudged toward finishing the purchase.

WHY THIS MATTERS

A shopper who's already carted something has done most of the work

Getting a brand-new visitor to the site costs money — ads, promotions, search placement. A shopper who's already put an item in their cart is a completely different situation: they've made most of the decision already. If we can tell which of those carts are worth a nudge — a reminder email, a discount, a retargeting ad — we can spend that effort where it actually pays off, instead of treating every abandoned cart the same.

That's the whole question this project set out to answer: given everything we know about how a shopper browsed, can we tell which carts are likely to convert — and which specific things about their behavior actually predict it?

WHAT WE FOUND

Five things worth knowing

1
Most of the drop-off happens before checkout, not during it

Once someone adds an item to their cart, roughly 4 in 5 of them go on to buy it. The real opportunity isn't fixing checkout — it's getting more of the right people to add something to cart in the first place.

2
We can tell which abandoned carts are worth a second chance

Of the 573,000 cart-containing visits in October, 50.9% converted. Looking closely at how someone shopped — not what they looked at, but how they browsed — we can meaningfully separate the likely buyers from the likely walk-aways. It's not perfect: about 1 in 5 "lost" carts look just as promising as a completed sale, so this is best used to prioritize follow-up, not as a certainty.

3
"How much" someone shops matters more than "what" they shop for

Time spent browsing and number of items carted are, by far, the strongest signals that someone's about to buy. Even when we set those aside and look only at what a shopper browsed — how many pages, what price range, how many different brands — we still find a real, useful pattern. That's the more actionable version: you can't design a feature that makes someone "browse longer," but you can design one that makes brand comparison easier or highlights the right price range.

4
Timing beats the day of the week

Buying activity swings 2.4x across the day — from a low around midnight UTC to a peak around 9am UTC. Day of the week barely matters by comparison (Monday's the best day, Tuesday the worst, and the gap between them is small). If Marketing is choosing when to send a promotion, the hour matters more than the day.

5
Black Friday brought the crowds, not the biggest sales day

Purchase volume clearly spiked on Black Friday, as expected. But the single highest-revenue day of November was actually 12 days earlier. Worth knowing before assuming Black Friday is automatically the day to plan the biggest push around.

HOW WE KNOW THIS HOLDS UP

We didn't just check October — we tested it against a second, harder month

Everything above was found by looking at October. Before trusting any of it, we checked whether the same patterns held true in November — a busier, more promotional month that includes Black Friday. They did: the tool that spots likely buyers worked just as well, if not slightly better, on a month it had never seen before.

One honest caveat: overall buying activity was a bit lower in November than October, and cart-to-purchase conversion specifically dropped noticeably during that busier month. That's a useful signal on its own — a shopper's cart means slightly less during a big promotional push than on a typical day, worth factoring into planning rather than assuming the numbers above hold identically year-round.

WHAT TO DO NEXT

Recommendations, by team

MARKETING
Use the "likely to buy" signal to prioritize who gets a cart-reminder email or retargeting ad first — don't treat every abandoned cart the same. Time promotional sends around the 7–10am UTC window rather than optimizing by day of week.
PRODUCT
Focus on getting more of the right shoppers to add something to cart in the first place — that's where the real upside is, not checkout. Make brand comparison and price browsing easier, since those are the specific things (besides time spent) that predict a sale.
CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE
Checkout itself is already working well — 4 in 5 carted items get bought. Put friction-reduction effort further upstream, in discovery and browsing, not the payment flow.
LEADERSHIP
Don't plan around Black Friday as automatically the biggest revenue day — check the data each year. And treat "carts convert less during big promotions" as a real, recurring pattern to plan for, not a one-off.
